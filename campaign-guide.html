Campaign Staff Handbook

How the four setup tabs turn a blank hex grid into a playable campaign — what each field controls, and what it stands for on the ground.

A campaign is assembled in four passes, each on its own tab, each building on the last. Think of it as writing the standing orders for a theatre of war before a single formation moves: first you fix the rules of the theatre, then you draw its ground, then you muster the armies that will fight over it, and only then do you actually open a campaign for players to join.

I Campaign Settings Campaign Scenarios ▸ Campaign Settings

Write the rulebook: map scale, march rates, weather, supply, sieges, courier risk, victory conditions.

II Campaign Map Campaign Scenarios ▸ Campaign Map

Paint the ground the rulebook will be applied to: terrain, roads, rivers, towns, depots.

III Campaign OB Campaign Scenarios ▸ Campaign OB

Muster the starting order of battle: formations, units and leaders, ready to be copied into any campaign.

IV New Campaign Start or Resume Campaign

Stamp out a live campaign from a finished template and set its clock running.

Why the split? Stages I–III author a reusable Campaign Scenario File (CSF) — a template of rules, map and starting order of battle. Stage IV then takes a snapshot of that template's map and OB and turns it into an actual, playable campaign. One CSF can be reused to launch many separate campaigns; editing a CSF after a campaign has been created from it does not retroactively change that campaign.
I

Campaign Settings

Campaign Scenarios ▸ Campaign Settings — authors a Campaign Scenario File (CSF)

This tab is the rulebook for the whole theatre: how big a hex is in real kilometres, how fast troops march across it, how the seasons and terrain slow them down, how armies stay fed, how sieges grind on, how orders and messages travel the chain of command, and how the war is ultimately scored. Everything here becomes the physics of the campaigns stamped from it.

Scenario — the essentials

FieldWhat it represents
NameThe CSF's own name, shown later in the CSF picker when someone creates a campaign.
Ground scalekm / hexReal-world distance one hex covers. This single number is what turns "movement rate" and "sighting distance" into a real geography — halve it and the same map becomes twice as large in ground truth.
Turn lengthhoursIn-world hours that pass per campaign turn. Combined with ground scale, this is what a "day's march" or a "forced march" actually means in hexes.
Max supply rangehexesHow far a hex may sit from a friendly supply source (depot, settlement, supply point) and still count as in-supply — the practical reach of a 19th-century supply line before wagons can't make the round trip in a turn.
Road move cost×The made road's own march speed, overriding whatever the hex's terrain would normally cost. Below 1 means marching on the road is faster than crossing open country — this is what makes road networks worth fighting for.
River crossing cost×Extra cost added when a march crosses a river edge — the time lost finding a ford, queuing at a bridge, or throwing a pontoon.
River blocks without crossingtoggleOn: an unbridged river is a hard stop — no ford, no crossing, full stop, like a river in flood. Off: it's merely slow and costly (see the multiplier above), like a river that can always be waded or swum at a price.
Supply points enabledtoggleMaster switch for mobile field depots — temporary supply dumps a formation can plant away from a town. Off: armies live only off fixed settlements and depots, the way a very short campaign or besieged theatre might.
Establish supply point coststandsThe manpower (in stands) a formation must spend from its own strength to build a field depot — digging in the wagon park and guarding it costs troops.
Max supply points per sideA cap on how many field depots each side can have running at once, representing finite wagon trains and engineer effort.
Horizontal comms ratehexes / turnHow fast a message travels sideways — between two formations of the same level, neither reporting up to the other. Represents liaison riders working corps-to-corps rather than through headquarters.
Horizontal contact rangehexesHow far apart two friendly formations can be and still talk to each other directly, without routing the message up and back down the chain of command.
Command order compliancetoggleOn: a subordinate can fail to fully carry out a command order and takes a strength penalty for it — friction, a commander reading the situation differently than his superior intended. Off: every order is obeyed to the letter.
Disobedience wastage%The strength a formation loses when it falls short of full compliance (only matters if the toggle above is on).
Courier search penalty×Extra time a rider spends hunting down a recipient who is on the move rather than sitting still — a moving target is harder to find than a fixed headquarters.

Strategic detectionoptional — blank = engine defaults

This is fog-of-war tuning, separate from the plain Sighting distances table above: it decides which terrain actually conceals troops, how much high ground helps a spotter, and how sighting degrades from a full, "I count two brigades of infantry" identification down to a partial "something is moving in that treeline" contact.

Leave the whole group blank and the engine falls back to sensible defaults (spelled out per field below) — you don't have to fill in every value just to touch one.

FieldWhat it represents
Open blocks LOStoggleWhen set, open on the traced sightline blocks it and conceals a formation standing in it. If none of the eight "blocks LOS" toggles below are set, the engine defaults to forest, urban, and walled village only — checking any one of these toggles replaces that whole default set with exactly the terrain types you've checked, it does not add to it.
Forest blocks LOStoggleWhen set, forest on the traced sightline blocks it and conceals a formation standing in it (tighter bands, no hill bonus). Part of the engine's default blocking set if nothing here is checked.
Hills blocks LOStoggleWhen set, hills on the traced sightline blocks it and conceals a formation standing in it — note this is separate from the hill spotting bonus below, which is about the observer's elevation, not the target's.
Marsh blocks LOStoggleWhen set, marsh on the traced sightline blocks it and conceals a formation standing in it.
Mountains blocks LOStoggleWhen set, mountains on the traced sightline blocks it and conceals a formation standing in it.
Urban blocks LOStoggleWhen set, urban on the traced sightline blocks it and conceals a formation standing in it. Part of the engine's default blocking set if nothing here is checked.
Walled village blocks LOStoggleWhen set, walled village on the traced sightline blocks it and conceals a formation standing in it. Part of the engine's default blocking set if nothing here is checked.
Water blocks LOStoggleWhen set, water on the traced sightline blocks it and conceals a formation standing in it.
Hill spotting bonushexesAdded to both the full and partial detection bands when the observer stands on hills or mountains; such an observer also sees over blocking terrain of equal-or-lower elevation tier (hills counts as tier 1, mountains as tier 2). Left blank/0: high ground gives no edge at all — a formation on a hilltop sees exactly as far as one on flat ground, and a tree line blocks it exactly the same way.
Partial sight range×The wider "presence only" detection band, as a multiple of the full-identification band. Blank ⇒ 1.5×. This only widens the "something's out there" warning radius — it never changes the full-identification range itself.
Obscured full bandhexesFixed full-identification range used instead of the normal formula when the TARGET formation stands in blocking terrain (forest/urban/etc, per the toggles above). The hill bonus never applies here — even a spotter on a mountain can't identify a formation hiding in cover beyond this fixed distance. Blank ⇒ 1 hex.
Obscured partial bandhexesFixed "presence only" range used the same way, when the target formation stands in blocking terrain. Blank ⇒ 2 hexes.
Static observer sighthexesFull-identification range for a static spotter — a friendly-controlled settlement or supply point, which scouts every turn as a fixed watchtower regardless of any formation standing there. Its partial band is this × Partial sight range ×. Blank ⇒ falls back to the Infantry sighting distance converted to hexes, or 2 hexes if that is also unset.
Selected vs. left blank, in practice: leaving every strategic-detection field blank plays with the engine's built-in fog of war (forest/urban/walled-village conceal troops, no hill advantage, tight 1–2 hex bands against hidden targets). Filling in values lets you loosen or tighten that fog for a specific theatre — e.g. raise the hill spotting bonus for an open, hilly map where high ground should matter more, or add hills/marsh to the blocking list for a scenario with heavy undergrowth. The one toggle to watch: checking even a single "blocks LOS" box turns off the engine's defaults entirely and uses only what you've checked, so if you want forest/urban/walled village to keep blocking and add another terrain, check all of them together.

Campaign Map

Campaign Scenarios ▸ Campaign Map — the ground this CSF's campaigns are fought over

This is the theatre itself: a rectangular hex grid you generate by row and column count, then hand-paint. Everything you place here is what "Ground scale" and the terrain-cost table from Stage I are actually describing — it's the map, this is the legend brought to life.

What you paint

ElementWhat it represents
TerrainPaint each hex with a terrain type from the CSF's own palette (open, forest, hills, marsh, mountains, urban, walled village, water) — the physical ground troops must cross and fight over.
RoadsPainted along hex edges. Not cosmetic — any edge you mark as a road gets the CSF's road-movement speed instead of the hex's normal terrain cost, so where you draw roads decides which routes armies actually prefer.
River edgesMarked along hex edges the same way as roads; these are what the CSF's river-crossing cost and "blocks without crossing" rule are keyed to.
SettlementsPlaced on a hex with a name, a type (village / town / city / fortress — feeds the Stage I siege tables), a flag marking it as a scenario objective (wired to control-settlement victory objectives), a flag marking it as a supply depot, and an owning side.
Supply pointsSeed field depots present from turn one, each owned by a side, with an "established turn" (normally turn 1) recording how long it's been supplying troops.
LabelsFree-text place names shown on the map for orientation — click a hex to add or edit one.
Like the CSF itself, this map is a template: every campaign created from this CSF gets its own private copy of it at creation. Editing the map afterward never changes a campaign that already exists.

After Creation

Still on Start or Resume Campaign — managing a campaign once it exists

Load a campaign you own from the Load existing… picker to inspect it read-only: its id, CSF id, start date, lifecycle Status (setup → active → completed) and current turn number.

Reassigning commanders

The Formations table lists every formation with its echelon, side, current hex, and commanding player. Type a player's user id into a row's box and press Set to hand that formation to them — or leave it blank to clear the seat. This is the one piece of campaign setup that genuinely can't happen earlier: a CSF's order-of-battle template has no players attached to it yet, only formations waiting for one.

Delete campaign is permanent and owner-only: it removes the campaign's map, formations, units, leaders, orders, and battle history outright. It does not touch the CSF it was created from — that template survives to launch other campaigns.
